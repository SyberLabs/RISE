# Oration on the Dignity of Man

Giovanni Pico della Mirandola, *Oratio de hominis dignitate* (1486). RISE translation, 2026, from the Latin.
Licence CC BY 4.0. A draft, served in RISE before the owner's reading (owner's decision, 2026-10-17); that reading verifies it.

Paragraph numbers (§) and sentence numbers follow the Pico Project numbering of the Latin in `oratio.la.md`.
Greek and Hebrew words are given in square brackets in the transliteration the Latin edition uses.
Translator's notes on uncertain readings are in `oration.notes.md`. Counts: 47 paragraphs, 11284 English words.

## § 1

1. I have read, most reverend Fathers, in the records of the Arabs, that Abdala the Saracen, when asked what on this stage of the world, so to speak, was to be seen as most worthy of wonder, answered that nothing could be seen more wonderful than man. 2. With this view agrees that saying of Mercury: "A great miracle, Asclepius, is man."

## § 2

3. As I thought over the reason for these sayings, I was not satisfied by the many things that many people bring forward about the excellence of human nature: that man is the go-between of creatures, familiar with those above, king of those below; by the keenness of his senses, the searching of his reason and the light of his intelligence, the interpreter of nature; the meeting-point between abiding eternity and flowing time; and (as the Persians say) the bond of the world, or rather its marriage; made, as David witnesses, a little lower than the angels.

## § 3

4. These are great things indeed, but not the chief ones, that is, not such as could rightly claim for themselves the privilege of the highest wonder. 5. For why should we not wonder more at the angels themselves and the most blessed choirs of heaven? 6. At last I seemed to myself to have understood why man is the most fortunate living creature, and therefore worthy of all wonder, and what, in the end, is that condition which fell to his lot in the order of the universe, a condition to be envied not only by the beasts but by the stars, and by the minds beyond the world. 7. A thing past belief, and wonderful. 8. And why not? For it is for this very reason that man is rightly called and thought a great miracle and a truly wonderful creature. 9. But hear, Fathers, what that condition is, and, in your courtesy, grant me a kind hearing for this work.

## § 4

10. The supreme Father, God the architect, had already built, by the laws of a hidden wisdom, this house of the world that we see, the most august temple of divinity. 11. He had adorned the region above the heavens with minds; he had brought the heavenly spheres to life with eternal souls; he had filled the dregs and refuse of the lower world with a throng of animals of every kind. 12. But when the work was finished, the craftsman wished there were someone to weigh the reason of so great a work, to love its beauty, and to wonder at its greatness. 13. So, when all things were now complete (as Moses and Timaeus bear witness), he last of all took thought of bringing forth man. 14. But there was nothing among the archetypes from which to shape a new offspring, nothing in the treasuries to bestow on a new son as his inheritance, and no seat in the whole world where this contemplator of the universe might sit. 15. Everything was now full; everything had been distributed among the highest, the middle and the lowest orders. 16. But it was not in keeping with a father's power to fail in its last offspring, as if worn out; it was not in keeping with wisdom to waver in a necessary matter for lack of counsel; it was not in keeping with generous love that one who was to praise divine generosity in others should be forced to condemn it in himself.

## § 5

17. At last the best of makers decreed that the one to whom nothing of his own could be given should share in whatever had been proper to each of the others. 18. So he took man, a work of no fixed image, and, setting him at the midpoint of the world, spoke to him thus: "We have given you, Adam, no fixed seat, no face of your own, no gift peculiar to you, so that whatever seat, whatever face, whatever gifts you yourself choose, these you may have and hold by your own wish and your own judgment. 19. The nature of all other things is defined and held within laws that we have laid down. 20. You, hemmed in by no narrow bounds, shall set the limits of your nature for yourself, by your own free choice, in whose hand I have placed you. 21. I have set you at the center of the world, so that from there you may more easily look around at whatever is in the world. 22. We have made you neither heavenly nor earthly, neither mortal nor immortal, so that, as the free and honoured shaper and molder of yourself, you may fashion yourself in whatever form you prefer. 23. You will be able to sink down into the lower things, which are the beasts; you will be able, by your soul's judgment, to be reborn into the higher things, which are divine."

## § 6

24. O the supreme generosity of God the Father, the supreme and wonderful happiness of man! 25. To him it is given to have what he chooses, to be what he wills. 26. The beasts, as soon as they are born, bring with them from their mother's bag (as Lucilius says) what they are going to possess. 27. The highest spirits were, either from the beginning or soon after, what they will be for everlasting ages. 28. In man at his birth the Father placed seeds of every sort and the shoots of every kind of life. 29. Whichever of these each man tends will grow up and bear their fruit in him. 30. If the vegetative ones, he will become a plant; if the sensitive, he will grow brutish; if the rational, he will come forth a heavenly creature; if the intellectual, he will be an angel and a son of God. 31. And if, content with the lot of no creature, he withdraws into the center of his own unity, made one spirit with God, in the solitary darkness of the Father, who is set above all things, he will stand before all things.

## § 7

32. Who would not wonder at this chameleon of ours? 33. Or who, indeed, would wonder at anything else more? 34. Not without reason did Asclepius the Athenian say that, on account of this shape-changing and self-transforming nature, man was signified in the mysteries by Proteus. 35. Hence those metamorphoses celebrated among the Hebrews and the Pythagoreans.

## § 8

36. For the more secret theology of the Hebrews, too, sometimes transforms holy Enoch into an angel of divinity, whom they call [malakh hasheckinah], and sometimes transforms others into other divine powers. 37. And the Pythagoreans deform wicked men into beasts and, if Empedocles is to be believed, even into plants. 38. Imitating them, Maumeth often had this saying on his lips, that whoever has turned away from the divine law becomes a beast; and rightly so. 39. For it is not the bark that makes a plant, but its dull and unfeeling nature; not the hide that makes a beast of burden, but its brute and sensual soul; not its round body that makes the heaven, but right reason; not separation from a body that makes an angel, but spiritual intelligence. 40. For if you see someone given over to his belly, crawling on the ground, it is a shrub you see, not a man; if you see someone blinded by the empty illusions of fantasy, as if by Calypso's, and, soothed by its tickling lure, made a slave to the senses, it is a beast you see, not a man. 41. If you see a philosopher discerning all things by right reason, revere him: he is a heavenly creature, not an earthly one. 42. If you see a pure contemplator, unaware of the body, withdrawn into the inner chambers of the mind, he is neither an earthly nor a heavenly creature: he is a more august divinity, clothed in human flesh.

## § 9

43. Who would not wonder at man? 44. Not without reason, in the sacred writings of Moses and of the Christians, he is named now "all flesh", now "every creature", since he shapes, builds and transforms himself into the look of all flesh and the character of every creature. 45. That is why Evantes the Persian, where he sets out the Chaldean theology, writes that man has no image of his own that is native to him, but many that are foreign and come from outside. 46. Hence that saying of the Chaldeans, [Enosh hu shinnuim vekammah tebhaoth baal haj], that is, man is a living creature of varied, many-formed and shifting nature.

## § 10

47. But to what end is all this? 48. So that we may understand that, since we were born on this condition, that we are what we will to be, we ought above all to take care that it may not be said of us that "when we were in honour we did not know it, and were made like the beasts and the senseless cattle." 49. But rather that saying of the prophet Asaph: "You are gods, and all sons of the Most High"; so that we do not abuse the most indulgent generosity of the Father and turn the free choice he gave us from a saving thing into a harmful one. 50. Let a certain holy ambition take hold of the mind, so that, not content with middling things, we pant after the highest, and strive with all our strength to reach them (since we can, if we will). 51. Let us disdain earthly things, despise heavenly ones, and, putting behind us at last whatever is of the world, fly to the court beyond the world, nearest to the most exalted divinity. 52. There, as the sacred mysteries teach, the Seraphim, Cherubim and Thrones hold the first places; let us, not knowing how to yield to them and unable to bear second place, rival both their dignity and their glory. 53. If we will it, we shall be in no way inferior to them.

## § 11

54. But in what way, or by doing what? 55. Let us see what they do, what life they live. 56. If we too live that life (for we can), we shall already have made our lot equal to theirs. 57. The Seraph burns with the fire of love; the Cherub shines with the brightness of intelligence; the Throne stands in the steadiness of judgment. 58. So if, given to the active life, we take up the care of lower things with right weighing, we shall be made firm with the settled solidity of the Thrones. 59. If, free from action, we busy ourselves in the leisure of contemplation, meditating on the maker in the work and the work in the maker, we shall flash on every side with Cherubic light. 60. If we burn with love for the maker alone, we shall suddenly be set ablaze by his consuming fire into the likeness of a Seraph. 61. Upon the Throne, that is, the just judge, sits God, judge of the ages. 62. Above the Cherub, that is, the contemplator, he flies, and warms him as if brooding over him. 63. For the spirit of the Lord moves over the waters, those, I mean, that are above the heavens, which in Job praise the Lord with hymns before dawn. 64. Whoever is a Seraph, that is, a lover, is in God and God in him; indeed, God and he are one. 65. Great is the power of the Thrones, which we attain by judging; highest the loftiness of the Seraphim, which we attain by loving.

## § 12

66. But how can anyone judge or love what he does not know? 67. Moses loved the God whom he saw, and as judge he carried out among the people what he had first seen as contemplator on the mountain. 68. So the Cherub in the middle, by his light, prepares us for the Seraphic fire and likewise lights our way to the judgment of the Thrones. 69. This is the bond of the first minds, the order of Pallas, the guardian of contemplative philosophy; this is what we must first rival and court, and so lay hold of, so that from there we may both be carried up to the heights of love and come down, well taught and ready, to the duties of action. 70. But it is well worth the effort, if our life is to be shaped on the model of the Cherubic life, to have before our eyes and ready to hand what that life is and of what kind, and what their actions and their works are. 71. Since we cannot reach this by ourselves, being flesh and having a taste for the things of the earth, let us go to the ancient fathers, who can give us the richest and surest testimony about these things, since they were at home in them and akin to them. 72. Let us ask the apostle Paul, the chosen vessel, what he saw the armies of the Cherubim doing when he was raised to the third heaven. 73. He will answer, with Dionysius as interpreter, that they are purified, then illuminated, and finally perfected.

## § 13

74. So let us too, rivalling the Cherubic life on earth, curbing the onrush of the passions by moral science, and scattering the fog of reason by dialectic, purify the soul, washing away, as it were, the filth of ignorance and vice, so that the passions do not rage at random, nor reason, losing its judgment, at times run wild. 75. Then let us flood the soul, now well ordered and cleansed, with the light of natural philosophy, so that at last we may perfect it with the knowledge of divine things.

## § 14

76. And so that we do not rely on ourselves alone, let us consult the patriarch Jacob, whose image shines carved on the seat of glory. 77. That wisest of fathers, who sleeps in the world below and wakes in the world above, will counsel us. 78. But he will counsel us through a figure (so did all things come to them): that there is a ladder stretching from the lowest ground to the heights of heaven, marked off in a series of many steps; that the Lord sits at the top, and that contemplating angels go up and down it by turns.

## § 15

79. If we, who aspire to the angelic life, must do this same thing, who, I ask, will touch the Lord's ladder with a soiled foot or with unwashed hands? 80. For the impure, as the mysteries hold, it is forbidden to touch the pure. 81. But what are these feet? 82. What are these hands? 83. Surely the foot of the soul is that most despised part by which it rests on matter as on the soil of the earth: the power, I mean, that nourishes and feeds, the tinder of lust and the teacher of soft pleasure. 84. And why should we not call anger the hands of the soul, anger which, as the champion of appetite, fights for it and, raiding in the dust and the sun, seizes what appetite will gorge on as it dozes in the shade? 85. These hands, these feet, that is, the whole sensual part, where the body's allurement sits and holds the soul, as they say, by the throat, let us wash in moral philosophy as in a living stream, so that we are not thrown off the ladder as profane and unclean. 86. But even this will not be enough, if we wish to be companions of the angels who run up and down Jacob's ladder, unless we have first been well fitted and trained to advance properly from step to step, never to stray from the path of the ladder, and to make the journeys there and back. 87. When we have achieved this through the art of speech, or of reasoning, then, quickened by a Cherubic spirit, philosophizing through the steps of the ladder, that is, of nature, and passing through all things from center to center, we shall at one time descend, tearing the one apart into the many with Titanic force, like Osiris, and at another ascend, gathering the many into one with the force of Phoebus, like the limbs of Osiris, until, resting at last in the bosom of the Father who is above the ladder, we are made perfect in theological happiness.

## § 16

88. Let us also question the just Job, who made a covenant with the God of life before he himself was brought into life, as to what the most high God most desires in those ten hundred thousand who stand before him: peace, surely, he will answer, in keeping with what we read in him: "He who makes peace in the high places." 89. And since the middle order interprets the counsels of the highest order to the lower ones, let the philosopher Empedocles interpret for us the words of Job the theologian. 90. He shows us a double nature set in our souls, by one of which we are lifted up to heavenly things, and by the other thrust down to the lower, through strife and friendship, or war and peace, as his poems bear witness. 91. In these he laments that, driven by strife and discord, like a madman, an exile from the gods, he is tossed upon the deep.

## § 17

92. Manifold indeed, Fathers, is the discord within us; we have grave wars inside our own house, worse than civil wars. 93. If we want to be rid of them, if we long for that peace which may lift us so high that we are set among the exalted of the Lord, philosophy alone will wholly restrain and calm them in us: first moral philosophy, if our man seeks only a truce from his enemies, will crush the unbridled raids of the many-formed beast and the brawls, rages and fury of the lion. 94. Then if, taking wiser counsel for ourselves, we desire the security of lasting peace, she will be at hand and will generously fulfil our wishes; for, with both beasts slain, like a sow struck down in sacrifice, she will seal an unbreakable covenant of most holy peace between flesh and spirit. 95. Dialectic will calm the disorders of reason, which is anxiously in turmoil among the contradictions of arguments and the snares of syllogisms. 96. Natural philosophy will calm the quarrels and disagreements of opinion, which harass, pull apart and tear the restless soul this way and that. 97. But it will calm them in such a way as to bid us remember that nature, according to Heraclitus, was born of war, and for that reason was called strife by Homer. 98. And so, it tells us, it cannot itself give us true quiet and solid peace within nature; this is the office and privilege of its mistress, that is, of most holy theology. 99. To her natural philosophy will both show the way and lead us as companion; and she, seeing us hurrying from afar, will call out: "Come to me, you who have laboured; come, and I will refresh you; come to me, and I will give you the peace that the world and nature cannot give you."

## § 18

100. So sweetly called, so kindly invited, flying on winged feet like earthly Mercuries into the embrace of our most blessed mother, we shall enjoy the peace we longed for: most holy peace, an undivided bond, a friendship of one soul, by which all minds, in the one mind that is above every mind, do not merely agree, but in some way past speaking become wholly one. 101. This is that friendship which the Pythagoreans say is the end of all philosophy; this is that peace which God makes in his high places, which the angels coming down to earth announced to men of good will, so that through it men, rising into heaven, might themselves become angels. 102. Let us wish this peace for our friends, wish it for our age, wish it for every house we enter, wish it for our own soul, so that through it the soul may become the house of God; so that, after it has shaken off its filth through moral philosophy and dialectic, adorned itself with manifold philosophy as with the furnishings of a court, and crowned the tops of its gates with the garlands of theology, the King of glory may come down and, coming with the Father, make his dwelling with it. 103. If the soul shows itself worthy of so great a guest, then, such is his boundless mercy, dressed in gilded clothing as in a wedding garment and wrapped in the manifold variety of the sciences, it will receive its beautiful guest no longer as a guest but as a bridegroom; and so as never to be parted from him, it will long to be parted from its own people and, forgetting its father's house and even itself, will long to die in itself so as to live in its bridegroom, in whose sight the death of his saints is precious indeed: that death, I say, if the fullness of life should be called death, of which the wise have said the study of philosophy is the rehearsal.

## § 19

104. Let us call on Moses himself as well, who falls only a little short of the fountain-fullness of sacred and unspeakable understanding, from whose nectar the angels drink themselves drunk. 105. We shall hear that venerable judge proclaiming laws to us, who live in the desert loneliness of this body: "Let those who are still unclean and in need of moral philosophy live with the common people outside the tabernacle, under the open sky, purifying themselves in the meantime like the priests of Thessaly. 106. Let those who have already set their conduct in order be received into the sanctuary, yet not handle the sacred things yet, but first, as zealous Levites of philosophy, serve the sacred rites in the service of dialectic. 107. Then, admitted to those rites themselves, let them contemplate in the priesthood of philosophy now the many-coloured adornment of the higher palace of God, that is, the starry adornment of his court, now the heavenly candlestick set out with seven lights, now the coverings of skin, the elements; so that at last, received through the merits of theological loftiness into the inner shrine of the temple, with no veil of image between, they may enjoy the glory of divinity." 108. This, surely, is what Moses commands us, and in commanding he warns, rouses and urges us to prepare for ourselves, through philosophy, while we can, the road to the heavenly glory to come.

## § 20

109. But indeed it is not only the Mosaic or Christian mysteries but also the theology of the ancients that shows us both the benefits and the dignity of these liberal arts which I have come here to dispute about. 110. For what else is meant by the grades of initiates kept in the secret rites of the Greeks, who were first purified through those arts which we have called, as it were, cleansing arts, moral philosophy and dialectic, and then were admitted to the mysteries? 111. And what else can that admission be but the interpretation of more hidden nature through philosophy? 112. Then at last, to those so prepared, came that [epopteia], that is, the beholding of divine things by the light of theology. 113. Who would not long to be initiated into such rites? 114. Who, putting all human things behind him, scorning the goods of fortune, neglecting those of the body, would not desire to become a guest at the gods' table while still living on earth, and, soaked in the nectar of eternity, to be given, though a mortal creature, the gift of immortality? 115. Who would not wish to be so breathed upon by those Socratic frenzies, sung by Plato in the Phaedrus, that, fleeing swiftly from here, that is, from the world, which is set in wickedness, by the rowing of wings and feet, he might be carried at the swiftest speed to the heavenly Jerusalem? 116. We shall be driven, Fathers, we shall be driven by Socratic frenzies, which will so set us outside our mind that they set our mind and ourselves in God. 117. We shall surely be driven by them if we have first done what is in our power; for if through moral philosophy the forces of the passions have been so tuned by due proportions to the measures that they sound together in steady harmony, and if through dialectic reason has moved forward in rhythm, then, roused by the frenzy of the Muses, we shall drink in the heavenly harmony with our inmost ears. 118. Then Bacchus, leader of the Muses, in his mysteries, that is, in the visible signs of nature, showing the invisible things of God to us as we philosophize, will make us drunk with the abundance of the house of God; and in that house, if like Moses we are faithful in all of it, most holy theology will come and fill us with a double frenzy. 119. For, raised to her loftiest watchtower, measuring from there in undivided eternity what is, what will be and what has been, and gazing up at the primal beauty, we shall be prophets of Phoebus for the one, winged lovers of the other; and at last, stirred as if by a gadfly by unspeakable love, burning like the Seraphim, set outside ourselves, full of divinity, we shall no longer be ourselves, but he himself who made us.

## § 21

120. The sacred names of Apollo, if one searches out their meanings and the mysteries hidden in them, show clearly enough that this god is no less a philosopher than a prophet. 121. Since Ammonius has dealt with this fully enough, there is no reason for me to treat it differently now; but let three Delphic precepts come to mind, Fathers, which are most necessary for those who are about to enter the most holy and august temple, not of a feigned but of the true Apollo, who lights every soul that comes into this world; you will see that they urge us to nothing else than to embrace with all our strength this threefold philosophy which is the subject of the present disputation. 122. For that [meden agan], that is, nothing too much, rightly prescribes the norm and rule of all the virtues by the principle of the mean, which moral philosophy treats. 123. Then that [gnothi seauton], that is, know yourself, rouses and urges us to the knowledge of the whole of nature, of which the nature of man is both the meeting-point and, as it were, the blend. 124. For whoever knows himself knows all things in himself, as Zoroaster first wrote, and then Plato in the Alcibiades. 125. Finally, lit by this knowledge through natural philosophy, and now close to God, saying [ei], that is, "you are", we shall address the true Apollo with the theological greeting, as friends, and therefore happily.

## § 22

126. Let us also consult Pythagoras, most wise, and wise chiefly for this reason, that he never thought himself worthy of the name of wise. 127. He will first instruct us not to sit on the bushel, that is, not to let go slack through idle sloth, and so lose, the rational part, by which the soul measures, judges and weighs all things, but to direct and rouse it constantly by the exercise and rule of dialectic. 128. Then he will point out to us two things to be guarded against above all: that we do not urinate facing the sun, and that we do not cut our nails during a sacrifice. 129. But once, through moral philosophy, we have passed out the loose appetites of overflowing pleasures, and cut off, like nail parings, the sharp points of anger and the stings of the passions, then at last let us begin to take part in the sacred rites, that is, in the mysteries of Bacchus we have mentioned, and to give ourselves to our contemplation, whose father and leader the sun is rightly called. 130. Finally, he will advise us to feed the cock, that is, to nourish the divine part of our soul with the knowledge of divine things, as with solid food and heavenly ambrosia. 131. This is the cock whose look the lion, that is, every earthly power, fears and reveres. 132. This is that cock to whom, we read in Job, understanding was given. 133. When this cock crows, the man who has gone astray comes back to his senses. 134. This cock, in the twilight of morning, when the morning stars praise God, joins in their song every day. 135. This is the cock that Socrates, dying, when he hoped to join the divinity of his soul to the divinity of the greater world, said he owed to Aesculapius, that is, to the physician of souls, now that he was beyond all danger of sickness.

## § 23

136. Let us review the records of the Chaldeans too, and we shall see (if they are to be believed) that the way to happiness lies open to mortals through these same arts. 137. The Chaldean interpreters write that it was a saying of Zoroaster that the soul is winged, and that when its wings fall away it is carried headlong into the body, and then, when they grow again, it flies back to the gods above. 138. When his disciples asked him how they might win souls fit for flight, with well-feathered wings, he said: "Water your wings with the waters of life." 139. When they asked again where they should look for these waters, he answered them with a parable (as was the man's way): "The paradise of God is washed and watered by four rivers. 140. From there draw your saving waters. 141. The name of the one from the north is [Pischon], which means the right; of the one from the west, [Gichon], which signifies expiation; of the one from the east, [Chiddekel], which means light; of the one from the south, [Perath], which we can translate as piety." 142. Turn your minds to this, Fathers, and consider carefully what these teachings of Zoroaster mean: surely nothing else than that we should wash the filth from our eyes with moral science, as with Iberian waters; and with dialectic, as with a northern rule, align their sight straight. 143. Then, in the contemplation of nature, we should accustom them to bear the still weak light of truth, like the first cradle of the rising sun, so that at last, through theological piety and the most holy worship of God, like heavenly eagles, we may bravely bear the dazzling radiance of the noonday sun. 144. These, perhaps, are those kinds of knowledge, of morning, of noon and of evening, first sung by David and explained more fully by Augustine. 145. This is that noonday light which sets the Seraphim fully ablaze and equally lights the Cherubim. 146. This is that region toward which the ancient father Abraham was always travelling. 147. This is that place where, as the teachings of both the Cabalists and the Moors have handed down, there is no place for unclean spirits. 148. And if it is lawful to bring before the public anything of the more secret mysteries, even under a riddle: since a sudden fall from heaven has struck the head of our man with dizziness, and, as Jeremiah says, death, coming in through the windows, has done harm to his liver and his breast, let us call on Raphael, the heavenly physician, to set us free with moral philosophy and dialectic as with healing medicines. 149. Then, when we are restored to good health, Gabriel, the strength of God, will dwell in us; leading us through the wonders of nature and showing us everywhere the virtue and power of God, he will at last hand us over to Michael, the high priest, who will mark those who have served out their time in the pay of philosophy with the priesthood of theology, as with a crown of precious stone.

## § 24

150. These, most reverend Fathers, are the things that have not only encouraged me toward the study of philosophy but compelled me to it. 151. I would certainly not have spoken of them, were I not answering those who are in the habit of condemning the study of philosophy, especially in men of high rank, or indeed in anyone who lives in modest fortune. 152. For by now all this philosophizing (such is the misfortune of our age) brings contempt and insult rather than honour and glory. 153. So deeply has this deadly and monstrous conviction taken hold of almost every mind, that philosophy should be pursued by no one, or by few. 154. As if to have before our eyes and in our hands, thoroughly searched out, the causes of things, the ways of nature, the reason of the universe, the counsels of God, the mysteries of heaven and earth, were nothing at all, unless one could hunt down some favour from it or get some profit for oneself. 155. Indeed, it has come to this, that now (what grief!) none are thought wise except those who make the study of wisdom a paid trade; so that one may see chaste Pallas, who dwells among men as a gift of the gods, thrown out, hooted off, hissed away, with no one to love her or stand by her, unless, as if selling herself and taking a small fee for her deflowered virginity, she brings the ill-gotten money back to her lover's purse.

## § 25

156. All this I say, not without the greatest grief and indignation, not against the princes but against the philosophers of this time, who both believe and preach that philosophy should not be pursued because no fee, no rewards, are set aside for philosophers; as if they were not showing, on this one count, that they themselves are not philosophers. 157. Since their whole life is set on gain or on ambition, they do not embrace the knowledge of truth for its own sake. 158. I will grant myself this, and will not blush at all to praise myself on this point: that I have never philosophized for any other reason than to philosophize, and that from my studies, from my long nights of work, I have neither hoped for nor sought any fee or fruit other than the cultivation of my mind and the knowledge of the truth, which I have always desired above all things. 159. I have always been so eager for it and so in love with it that, leaving aside every care of private and public affairs, I have given myself wholly to the leisure of contemplation; and from it no disparagement by the envious, no abuse from the enemies of wisdom, has been able to deter me until now, or will be able to in the future. 160. Philosophy herself has taught me to depend on my own conscience rather than on the judgments of others, and always to take care not so much that I am not ill spoken of as that I do not myself say or do anything ill.

## § 26

161. I was certainly not unaware, most reverend Fathers, that this very disputation of mine, pleasing and delightful as it would be to all of you who favour good learning and have wished to honour it with your most august presence, would be heavy and irksome to many others; and I know there is no lack of people who have condemned my undertaking before now, and condemn it at present on many counts. 162. For it is usual for things done well and holily, for the sake of virtue, to have no fewer barking critics, not to say more, than things done wickedly and wrongly, for the sake of vice. 163. There are some who do not approve of this whole kind of disputation, or of this custom of debating learned matters in public, asserting that it serves rather for the display of talent and learning than for gaining knowledge. 164. There are others who do not disapprove of this kind of exercise as such, but in no way approve of it in me, because I, at my age, just twenty-four years old, have dared to propose a disputation on the lofty mysteries of Christian theology, on the deepest questions of philosophy, on unknown disciplines, in a most famous city, before a very large gathering of most learned men, in the apostolic senate. 165. Others, granting me that I may dispute, will not grant that I may dispute on nine hundred questions, slandering this as done as needlessly and ambitiously as it was beyond my strength. 166. I would have given in to their objections at once, if the philosophy I profess had taught me so; and even now, since she teaches me so, I would not answer them, if I believed that this debate among us had been set up for the purpose of quarrelling and brawling. 167. So let every intent to disparage and provoke go from our minds, and let envy go too, which Plato writes is always absent from the divine choir; and let us look into, as friends, whether I should dispute, and whether on so many questions.

## § 27

168. First, then, to those who slander this custom of public disputation I shall not say much, since this fault, if it is judged a fault, I share not only with all of you, most excellent doctors, who have often performed this office, not without the highest praise and glory, but with Plato, with Aristotle, and with the most approved philosophers of every age. 169. They held it most certain that nothing served them better for reaching the knowledge of truth they sought than to engage very often in the exercise of disputation. 170. For just as the strength of the body is made firmer by gymnastics, so without doubt, in this wrestling school of letters, as it were, the strength of the mind grows far stronger and livelier. 171. Nor would I believe that either the poets, by the celebrated arms of Pallas, or the Hebrews, when they say that [barzel], iron, is the symbol of the wise, meant to signify to us anything other than contests of this kind, most honourable and altogether necessary for gaining wisdom. 172. Which is perhaps why the Chaldeans too, in the horoscope of one who is to be a philosopher, require that Mars should look on Mercury in triangular aspect; as if, should you take away these encounters, these wars, all philosophy would become sleepy and drowsy.

## § 28

173. But with those who say I am unequal to this task, my defense is harder: for if I say I am equal to it, I shall perhaps seem to bring on myself the mark of a man immodest and with too high an opinion of himself; if I admit I am unequal, the mark of one rash and thoughtless. 174. See into what straits I have fallen, in what position I am placed, since I cannot without fault promise of myself what, soon after, I cannot without fault fail to deliver. 175. Perhaps I could also cite that saying of Job, that there is a spirit, a spirit in all, and hear with Timothy: "Let no one despise your youth." 176. But I will say this more truly, from my own conscience: that there is nothing great or singular in me; I do not deny that I am perhaps studious and eager for good learning, but the name of learned I neither take nor claim for myself. 177. So if I have laid so great a burden on my shoulders, it was not because I was unaware of my weakness, but because I knew that in this kind of fight, that is, in fights of learning, there is this peculiar thing: that to be beaten is a gain. 178. So it is that even the weakest may rightly, and should, not only not shrink from such fights but seek them out of their own accord. 179. For the one who goes down receives from the victor a benefit, not an injury, since through him he goes home richer, that is, more learned, and better prepared for future fights. 180. Spurred by this hope, I, a weak soldier, have not been at all afraid to fight so heavy a battle with the strongest and most vigorous of all. 181. Whether this was done rashly or not, however, can surely be judged more rightly from the outcome of the battle than from my age.

## § 29

182. It remains for me, in the third place, to answer those who are offended by the sheer number of the propositions put forward, as if this burden sat on their shoulders, and not rather as if this labour, however great it is, had to be borne by me alone. 183. Surely it is unseemly and far too fussy to want to set a limit to another man's industry and, as Cicero says, to want moderation in a thing that is the better the greater it is. 184. In so great a venture it was necessary for me either to fail or to make good; if I made good, I do not see why what is praiseworthy to achieve in ten questions should be thought blameworthy to have achieved in nine hundred as well. 185. If I failed, they will have, if they hate me, grounds to accuse me, and, if they love me, grounds to excuse me. 186. For a young man to have failed in so grave and so great a matter, with slender talent and little learning, will deserve pardon rather than accusation. 187. Indeed, as the poet says: "If strength fails, daring will surely earn praise; in great things it is enough to have willed." 188. And if in our age many, imitating Gorgias of Leontini, have been accustomed, not without praise, to propose disputation not only on nine hundred questions but on all the questions of all the arts, why may I not, and without blame, dispute on many questions indeed, but definite and fixed ones?

## § 30

189. But this, they say, is superfluous and ambitious. 190. I, on the contrary, maintain that I did this not superfluously but of necessity; and if they considered the method of philosophizing with me, they too would be forced to admit it plainly, however unwilling. 191. For those who have bound themselves to one of the schools of philosophers, following, say, Thomas or Scotus, who are now most in hand, can indeed put their doctrine to the test in the discussion of a few questions. 192. But I have so trained myself that, sworn to no one's words, I have spread myself through all the masters of philosophy, shaken out every page, and come to know every school. 193. So, since I had to speak of all of them, lest by defending a single doctrine and setting the rest aside I should seem bound to it, there could not fail to be very many propositions brought forward about all of them together, even if only a few were proposed about each. 194. Nor let anyone condemn me for this, that wherever the storm carries me, I land there as a guest. 195. For it was the practice of all the ancients, in reading through every kind of writer, to leave no commentaries they could find unread, and most of all of Aristotle, who for that reason was called by Plato [anagnostes], that is, the reader; and surely it is the mark of a narrow mind to have shut itself up within a single Porch or Academy. 196. Nor can anyone rightly choose his own from among them all who has not first come to know them all well. 197. Add that in each school there is something distinctive that it does not share with the others.

## § 31

198. And to begin now with our own, to whom philosophy came last: there is in John Scotus something lively and incisive; in Thomas, something solid and even; in Giles, something neat and exact; in Francis, something sharp and keen; in Albert, something ancient, broad and grand; in Henry, as it has seemed to me, something always lofty and venerable. 199. Among the Arabs, there is in Averroes something firm and unshaken; in Avempace and in Alpharabius, something grave and well considered; in Avicenna, something divine and Platonic. 200. Among the Greeks philosophy is, in general, polished, and above all pure; in Simplicius it is rich and abundant, in Themistius elegant and concise, in Alexander consistent and learned, in Theophrastus worked out with weight, in Ammonius smooth and graceful. 201. And if you turn to the Platonists, to review just a few: in Porphyry you will delight in the wealth of matter and a many-sided religion; in Iamblichus you will revere a more secret philosophy and the mysteries of the barbarians; in Plotinus there is no single thing for you to admire, for he offers himself to be admired everywhere; when he speaks divinely of divine things, and of human things far above the human, with a learned obliquity of speech, the Platonists understand him only with sweat. 202. I pass over the more recent ones, Proclus, luxuriant with Asian richness, and those who flowed from him, Hermias, Damascius, Olympiodorus and many others, in all of whom that [to theion], that is, the divine, the particular mark of the Platonists, always shines out.

## § 32

203. Besides, if there is any school that attacks the truer doctrines and makes sport of good causes with the slanders of its wit, it strengthens the truth rather than weakening it, and, like a flame shaken by motion, it stirs the truth up rather than putting it out. 204. Moved by this reasoning, I wished to bring into the open the tenets not of one doctrine only (as some would have liked), but of every kind of doctrine, so that by this comparison of many schools and this discussion of a manifold philosophy, that radiance of truth which Plato mentions in his Letters might shine more clearly on our minds, like the sun rising from on high. 205. What would it have been worth if only the philosophy of the Latins had been treated, that is, of Albert, Thomas, Scotus, Giles, Francis and Henry, and the philosophers of the Greeks and the Arabs had been left out? 206. For all wisdom flowed from the barbarians to the Greeks, and from the Greeks to us. 207. So our own people, in their method of philosophizing, have always relied on the discoveries of foreigners, and thought it enough to have cultivated what belonged to others. 208. What would it have been worth to deal with natural things together with the Peripatetics, unless the Academy of the Platonists were called in as well, whose doctrine, even concerning divine things, has always been held, as Augustine witnesses, the holiest of all philosophies, and which I now, for the first time as far as I know (may the claim give no offense), after many centuries, have brought before the public to be tested in disputation? 209. And what would it have been worth to have treated the opinions of others, however many they were, if, coming to the banquet of the wise without our share, we brought nothing that was our own, born and worked out by our own mind? 210. Surely it is ignoble (as Seneca says) to be wise only out of a notebook, as if the discoveries of our elders had closed the road to our own industry, as if the force of nature were spent in us, so that it could bring forth nothing of itself that, if it did not prove the truth, would at least point toward it, even from far away. 211. And if the farmer hates barrenness in his field, and the husband in his wife, surely the divine mind, joined and bound to a barren soul, will hate it all the more, as the offspring it longs for from that soul is far nobler.

## § 33

212. For this reason I was not content to have added, besides the common doctrines, much on the ancient theology of Mercury Trismegistus, much on the teachings of the Chaldeans and of Pythagoras, much on the more secret mysteries of the Hebrews; I have also proposed for disputation a great many things about natural and divine matters that I discovered and thought out myself.

## § 34

213. I proposed, first, the concord of Plato and Aristotle, believed by many before now, adequately proven by no one. Boethius, who among the Latins promised he would do it, is not found ever to have done what he always wished to do. 214. Simplicius, who among the Greeks professed the same, would that he delivered it as fully as he promises it. 215. Augustine too writes in the Academics that there were many who tried, in their most subtle disputations, to prove the same thing, namely that the philosophy of Plato and that of Aristotle are the same. 216. John the Grammarian likewise, though he says that Plato differs from Aristotle only for those who do not understand Plato's words, nonetheless left the proof of it to those who came after. 217. I have also added a number of passages in which I maintain that the opinions of Scotus and Thomas, and a number in which those of Averroes and Avicenna, which are thought to be at odds, are in agreement.

## § 35

218. In the second place, I set out what I myself have thought out in philosophy, both Aristotelian and Platonic, and then seventy-two new propositions in physics and metaphysics; whoever holds them will be able, unless I am mistaken (and that will soon be clear to me), to resolve any question proposed about natural and divine things by a method very different from the one taught us by the philosophy that is read in the schools and cultivated by the doctors of this age. 219. Nor should anyone, Fathers, be so much surprised that I, in my first years, at a tender age, at which I could barely (as some keep saying) have read the commentaries of others, should wish to bring forward a new philosophy, as he should rather either praise it if it is defended, or condemn it if it is refuted; and finally, since they are going to judge these discoveries and these writings of mine, they should count not the author's years but rather their merits or their faults.

## § 36

220. Besides that philosophy, there is another that I have brought forward: a new method of philosophizing through numbers, though an ancient one, observed by the ancient theologians, above all by Pythagoras, by Aglaophenus, by Philolaus, by Plato and the earlier Platonists. 221. But in our time, like other fine things, it has so fallen out of use through the neglect of later generations that hardly any traces of it can be found. 222. Plato writes in the Epinomis that among all the liberal arts and contemplative sciences the science of numbering is the chief and the most divine. 223. Asking, likewise, why man is the wisest animal, he answers: "Because he knows how to count." 224. Aristotle too recalls this view in the Problems. 225. Abumasar writes that it was a saying of Avenzoar of Babylon that he who knew how to count knew all things. 226. These things can in no way be true if by the art of numbering they meant the art in which merchants are now the most skilled of all; as Plato too bears witness, warning us in plain words not to take this divine arithmetic for the arithmetic of merchants. 227. Since, then, after many nights of study, I seem to myself to have explored that arithmetic which is so highly praised, I have publicly promised, to put the matter to the test, to answer through numbers seventy-four questions that are held to be among the chief questions of physics and of theology.

## § 37

228. I have also proposed magical theorems, in which I have shown that there are two kinds of magic: one consists wholly in the work and authority of demons, a thing, so help me God, accursed and monstrous. 229. The other, when well examined, is nothing other than the complete fulfilment of natural philosophy. 230. The Greeks, mentioning both, call the first [goeteian], in no way thinking it worthy of the name of magic, and the second by its own proper name, [mageian], as if to call it the perfect and highest wisdom. 231. For, as Porphyry says, magus in the Persian tongue means the same as an interpreter and worshipper of divine things among us. 232. Great, indeed very great, Fathers, is the difference and the unlikeness between these arts. 233. The first is condemned and cursed not only by the Christian religion but by all laws, by every well-ordered state. 234. The second is approved and embraced by all the wise, by all nations devoted to the study of heavenly and divine things. 235. The first is the most deceitful of the arts; the second, a higher and holier philosophy; the first is fruitless and empty, the second firm, trustworthy and solid. 236. Whoever practised the first always hid it, because it brought shame and disgrace on its practitioner; from the second, in ancient times and almost always, the highest renown and glory in learning was sought. 237. No man who was a philosopher and eager to learn good arts was ever a student of the first; to learn the second, Pythagoras, Empedocles, Democritus and Plato crossed the seas; they preached it on their return and held it chief among their secrets. 238. The first, just as it is supported by no reasons, so it is supported by no reliable authors; the second, honoured as if by most illustrious parents, has two chief founders: Xamolsis, whom Abbaris the Hyperborean imitated, and Zoroaster, not the one you may be thinking of, but the son of Oromasius. 239. If we ask Plato what the magic of each of them is, he will answer in the Alcibiades that the magic of Zoroaster is nothing other than the science of divine things, in which the kings of the Persians had their sons taught, so that they might learn to rule their own commonwealth on the model of the commonwealth of the world. 240. He will answer in the Charmides that the magic of Xalmosis is the medicine of the soul, through which, that is, temperance is won for the soul, as health is won for the body through the other medicine.

## § 38

241. In their footsteps there followed later Carondas, Damigeron, Apollonius, Hostanes and Dardanus. 242. So did Homer, who, as he did with all other kinds of wisdom, concealed this one too beneath the wanderings of his Ulysses, as I shall one day prove in my poetic theology. 243. So did Eudoxus and Hermippus. 244. So did nearly all who searched into the Pythagorean and Platonic mysteries. 245. Among the more recent, I find three who caught its scent: Alchindus the Arab, Roger Bacon and William of Paris. 246. Plotinus too mentions it, where he shows that the magus is the servant of nature and not its craftsman; that wisest of men approves and upholds this magic, while he so abhors the other that, when he was summoned to the rites of evil demons, he said it was more fitting for them to come to him than for him to go to them; and rightly so. 247. For as the first makes man the subject and slave of wicked powers, so the second makes him their prince and lord. 248. The first, in short, cannot claim for itself the name of either art or science; the second, full of the loftiest mysteries, embraces the deepest contemplation of the most secret things and, in the end, the knowledge of the whole of nature. 249. Calling into the light, as if from their hiding places, the powers scattered and sown in the world by the kindness of God, it does not so much work wonders as busily serve nature as she works them. 250. Having searched more closely and inwardly into the harmony of the universe, which the Greeks more expressively call [sumpatheian], and having come to know well the mutual kinship of natures, applying to each thing its native lures, which are called the [iunges] of the magi, it brings out into the open the wonders hidden in the recesses of the world, in the lap of nature, in the storehouses and secrets of God, as if it were itself their maker; and as the farmer weds elms to vines, so the magus weds earth to heaven, that is, the lower things to the gifts and powers of the higher. 251. So it is that the first appears as monstrous and harmful as the second appears divine and saving. 252. Chiefly for this reason: the first, enslaving man to the enemies of God, calls him away from God; the second rouses him to that wonder at the works of God from which a ready love, faith and hope most surely follow. 253. For nothing moves us more to religion and to the worship of God than constant contemplation of the wonders of God; and once we have explored them well through this natural magic we are speaking of, fired more ardently to the worship and love of the maker, we shall be compelled to sing: "The heavens are full, all the earth is full of the majesty of your glory." 254. And this is enough about magic; I have said this much about it because I know there are many who, as dogs always bark at strangers, likewise often condemn and hate what they do not understand.

## § 39

255. I come now to the things I have brought forward, dug out of the ancient mysteries of the Hebrews, to confirm the holy and catholic faith; and so that they are not thought, by those to whom they are unknown, to be made-up trifles or the tales of peddlers, I want everyone to understand what they are and of what kind, where they were sought, by what authors and how illustrious they are confirmed, and how hidden, how divine, how necessary they are for our people in defending religion against the persistent slanders of the Hebrews.

## § 40

256. Not only the famous doctors of the Hebrews but also, among our own, Hesdras, Hilary and Origen write that Moses received on the mountain from God not only the law, which he left to posterity written down in five books, but also a more secret and true explanation of the law; and that he was commanded by God to make the law public to the people, but neither to commit the interpretation of the law to writing nor to spread it abroad, but to reveal it only to Iesu Nave, and Iesu Nave in turn to the leading priests who came after, under a great and solemn duty of silence. 257. It was enough, through the plain story, to recognize now the power of God, now his anger toward the wicked, his mercy toward the good, his justice toward all, and, through divine and saving precepts, to be trained to live well and happily and in the worship of true religion. 258. But to make plain to the common people the more secret mysteries, hidden under the bark of the law and the rough cloak of its words, the secrets of the highest divinity, what would that have been but to give what is holy to dogs and to cast pearls among swine?

## § 41

259. So to keep these things hidden from the crowd, to be shared with the perfect, among whom alone Paul says he speaks wisdom, was not a matter of human counsel but of divine command. 260. The ancient philosophers kept this custom most religiously. 261. Pythagoras wrote nothing except a few small things, which he entrusted to his daughter Dama as he was dying. 262. The Sphinxes carved on the temples of the Egyptians gave this warning: that mystical doctrines should be kept untouched by the profane crowd through the knots of riddles. 263. Plato, writing to Dionysius about the highest substances, says: "It must be said in riddles, so that if the letter should happen to fall into others' hands, what I write to you may not be understood by others." 264. Aristotle used to say that the books of the Metaphysics, in which he treats divine things, were published and not published. 265. Why say more? Origen asserts that Jesus Christ, the teacher of life, revealed many things to his disciples which they did not wish to write down, lest they become common to the crowd. 266. This is strongly confirmed by Dionysius the Areopagite, who says that the more secret mysteries were passed on by the founders of our religion [ek nou eis noun dia meson logon], that is, from mind to mind, without writing, by the spoken word in between. 267. In exactly this same way, when by God's command that true interpretation of the law, handed down to Moses from God, was revealed, it was called Cabala, which means among the Hebrews what "receiving" means among us; for this reason, namely, that one received that doctrine from another, not through written records but through an ordered succession of revelations, as if by right of inheritance.

## § 42

268. But after the Hebrews had been restored from the Babylonian captivity by Cyrus, and the temple had been rebuilt under Zorobabel, and they had turned their minds to restoring the law, Esdras, then head of the congregation, after he had corrected the book of Moses, saw plainly that, because of the exiles, slaughters, flights and captivity of the people of Israel, the custom set by their ancestors of handing down the doctrine from hand to hand could not be kept, and that the secrets of the heavenly doctrine granted to them by God would perish, since their memory could not last long without written commentaries; so he decided that the wise men who were still left should be called together, that each should bring forward what he held in memory of the mysteries of the law, and that, with scribes brought in, these should be set down in seventy volumes (for there were about that many wise men in the Sanhedrin). 269. And so that you need not take my word alone on this, Fathers, hear Esdras himself speaking thus: "When forty days were over, the Most High spoke, saying: 'Make public the first things you have written, and let the worthy and the unworthy read them; but keep the last seventy books, to hand them on to the wise among your people. 270. For in them is the vein of understanding, and the fountain of wisdom, and the river of knowledge.' 271. And so I did." 272. These are Esdras's words, word for word. 273. These are the books of the science of Cabala; in these books, Esdras rightly proclaimed in a very clear voice, are the vein of understanding, that is, an unspeakable theology of the supersubstantial deity; the fountain of wisdom, that is, an exact metaphysics of the intelligible and angelic forms; and the river of knowledge, that is, a most firm philosophy of natural things.

## § 43

274. Sixtus the Fourth, Supreme Pontiff, the immediate predecessor of Innocent the Eighth, under whom we now happily live, took the greatest care and pains to have these books put into Latin for the public benefit of our faith. 275. And by the time he died, three of them had already reached the Latins. 276. Among the Hebrews these books are held at this time in such reverence that no one under forty years of age is allowed to touch them. 277. When I had obtained these books at no small expense, and had read them through with the greatest care and unwearied labour, I saw in them (God is my witness) a religion not so much Mosaic as Christian. 278. There is the mystery of the Trinity, there the incarnation of the Word, there the divinity of the Messiah; there I have read, on original sin, on its atonement through Christ, on the heavenly Jerusalem, on the fall of the demons, on the orders of the angels, on purgatories, on the punishments of hell, the same things we read every day in Paul and Dionysius, in Jerome and Augustine. 279. And in what concerns philosophy, you would think you were hearing Pythagoras and Plato outright, whose teachings are so akin to the Christian faith that our Augustine gives immense thanks to God that the books of the Platonists came into his hands.

## § 44

280. In short, there is hardly any matter of controversy between us and the Hebrews on which they cannot be so refuted and convinced from the books of the Cabalists that not even a corner is left for them to hide in. 281. Of this I have a most weighty witness in Antonius Cronicus, a most learned man, who, when I was at a banquet at his house, heard with his own ears Dactylus, a Hebrew skilled in this science, come over, hand and foot, entirely to the Christians' view of the Trinity.

## § 45

282. But to return to reviewing the heads of my disputation: I have also brought forward my view on interpreting the songs of Orpheus and Zoroaster. 283. Orpheus is read among the Greeks almost complete; Zoroaster among them only in fragments, among the Chaldeans more complete; both are believed to be fathers and founders of ancient wisdom. 284. For, to say nothing of Zoroaster, who is often mentioned among the Platonists, and always with the highest veneration, Iamblichus of Chalcis writes that Pythagoras held the Orphic theology as the model on which to shape and form his own philosophy. 285. Indeed, they say that the sayings of Pythagoras are called sacred for this reason alone, that they flowed from the teachings of Orpheus; from there came the secret doctrine of numbers, and whatever was great and lofty in Greek philosophy, as from its first source. 286. But (as was the custom of the ancient theologians) Orpheus so wove the mysteries of his teachings into the wrappings of fables and disguised them with a poetic veil that anyone who reads his hymns would think there was nothing beneath them but stories and the purest nonsense. 287. I wished to say this so that it may be known what labour, what difficulty I had in digging out the meanings of a secret philosophy hidden in the deliberate tangles of riddles and the hiding places of fables, especially since in a matter so grave, so hidden and unexplored, I was helped by no work or care of other interpreters. 288. And yet my dogs have barked that I heaped up trifling and light matters to make a show of numbers; as if I had not brought forward all the most doubtful and disputed questions, over which the leading schools fight it out; as if I had not brought to these very men, who both pick at my work and believe themselves the princes of philosophers, many things wholly unknown and untried.

## § 46

289. Indeed, I am so far from that fault that I took care to compress the disputation into as few heads as I could. 290. If I had wished (as others are accustomed to do) to divide it into its parts and cut it to pieces, it would surely have grown to a countless number. 291. And, to say nothing of the rest, who does not know that a single one of the nine hundred propositions, namely the one on reconciling the philosophy of Plato and Aristotle, I could, without any suspicion of seeking numbers for show, have drawn out into six hundred heads, not to say more, by listing one by one all the places in which others think they disagree and I think they agree? 292. But surely (for I will say it, though neither modestly nor in keeping with my own nature), I will say it nonetheless, because the envious force me to say it, the detractors force me: I wished by this encounter of mine to give proof not so much that I knew many things, as that I knew things that many do not know.

## § 47

293. So that this may now be made plain to you by the event itself, most reverend Fathers, and so that my speech may no longer delay your desire, most excellent doctors, whom I see, not without great pleasure, ready and girded, awaiting the fight: let us now, and may it be happy and fortunate, join battle, as at the call of the trumpet.
